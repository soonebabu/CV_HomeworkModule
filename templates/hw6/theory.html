Theory and derivations

1. Optical flow from brightness constancy

Let $I(x, y, t)$ be the image intensity. A scene point that is at $(x, y)$ at time $t$ moves to $(x + \delta x,\, y + \delta y)$ at time $t + \delta t$. The basic assumption is brightness constancy: the point keeps its brightness while it moves,

$$ I(x + \delta x,\; y + \delta y,\; t + \delta t) = I(x, y, t). $$

For small motion, expand the left side in a first-order Taylor series:

$$ I(x, y, t) + \frac{\partial I}{\partial x}\delta x + \frac{\partial I}{\partial y}\delta y + \frac{\partial I}{\partial t}\delta t + \text{h.o.t.} = I(x, y, t). $$

Cancel $I(x,y,t)$, divide by $\delta t$ and let $\delta t \to 0$. With $u = dx/dt$, $v = dy/dt$ (the optical flow) and $I_x, I_y, I_t$ the partial derivatives:

$$ I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \nabla I^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{v} = -I_t . \label{ofce} $$

This is the optical flow constraint equation. It is one equation in two unknowns per pixel, so it only fixes the component of the motion along the gradient (the normal flow),

$$ v_\perp = \frac{-I_t}{\lVert \nabla I \rVert}, $$

while the component along the edge is invisible - the aperture problem. You can see it in the flow videos: on a flat, textureless wall or a clear sky the flow is zero even when the camera pans, because there's no gradient to see the motion with. Every flow method adds an extra assumption to fix this. Lucas-Kanade (section 3) assumes the flow is constant in a small window. Farneback, which part 1 uses for dense flow, approximates each neighbourhood by a quadratic polynomial $f(\mathbf{x}) \approx \mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x} + c$. If the second frame is the first one shifted by $\mathbf{d}$, then $f_2(\mathbf{x}) = f_1(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{d})$, so $A_2 = A_1$ and $\mathbf{b}_2 = \mathbf{b}_1 - 2A_1\mathbf{d}$. That gives $\mathbf{d} = -\tfrac12 A_1^{-1}(\mathbf{b}_2 - \mathbf{b}_1)$ at every pixel, solved in a window and coarse-to-fine on a pyramid.

2. What optical flow tells us

Take a pinhole camera with focal length $f$ moving through a static scene with translational velocity $(V_x, V_y, V_z)$ and angular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega$. A scene point at depth $Z$, seen at image position $(x, y)$ (measured from the principal point), has image flow (Longuet-Higgins & Prazdny)

$$ \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{Z}\begin{bmatrix} -f & 0 & x \\ 0 & -f & y \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} V_x \\ V_y \\ V_z \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} \frac{xy}{f} & -\left(f + \frac{x^2}{f}\right) & y \\ f + \frac{y^2}{f} & -\frac{xy}{f} & -x \end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol\omega . $$

An object moving in front of a still camera produces the same flow with the signs of its velocity reversed. Almost everything the flow page reports comes out of this equation:

  • Where and when something moves - thresholding $\lVert\mathbf v\rVert$ segments the moving pixels in every frame (the red mask, the "% of pixels moving" curve, the heat map of average motion). This is motion detection/segmentation without any model of what the object is.
  • Direction and speed - $\operatorname{atan2}(v, u)$ is the direction on screen (the colour wheel and the direction histogram) and $\lVert\mathbf v\rVert$ in px/frame times the frame rate is px/s. It only becomes m/s with depth: for motion parallel to the image plane, $V \approx \lVert\mathbf v\rVert\, Z / f$.
  • Camera motion vs. object motion - the rotational term doesn't depend on $Z$ and is smooth over the whole image, and camera translation produces a field that is smooth wherever depth is. So we fit a global affine field $u = a_0 + a_1x + a_2y$, $v = b_0 + b_1x + b_2y$ to every frame:
    • $(a_0, b_0)$ is a pan/tilt (content moves opposite to the direction the camera turns);
    • the divergence $\nabla\cdot\mathbf v = a_1 + b_2$ is positive when the field expands - the camera moves forward or something approaches (pure $V_z$ gives $u = xV_z/Z$, $v = yV_z/Z$, so $\nabla\cdot\mathbf v = 2V_z/Z$ and the time to contact is $Z/V_z = 2/\nabla\cdot\mathbf v$ without knowing $Z$ or $V_z$ separately);
    • the curl $b_1 - a_2$ is rotation about the viewing axis (camera roll).
    Whatever the global field doesn't explain is independent motion - things moving on their own.
  • Relative depth - for a translating camera the flow is proportional to $1/Z$, so near objects move faster across the image than far ones (motion parallax). In a walking or panning video the heat map of flow magnitude is roughly an inverse-depth map.
  • Boundaries - discontinuities in the flow field mark occlusion boundaries (edges of moving objects), even when the colours on both sides are similar.

3. Tracking equations for two frames (Lucas-Kanade)

Setting up the problem

Take two consecutive frames, $I(\mathbf x) = I(x, y, t)$ and $J(\mathbf x) = I(x, y, t+1)$, and a feature at pixel $\mathbf u = (u_x, u_y)$ in $I$. Tracking means finding the displacement $\mathbf d = (d_x, d_y)$ so that $\mathbf u + \mathbf d$ in $J$ is "the same point". One pixel gives only one equation (the aperture problem), so assume every pixel in a small window $W$ around $\mathbf u$ (21 × 21 in the code) moves by the same $\mathbf d$, and choose $\mathbf d$ to minimise the sum of squared differences:

$$ \varepsilon(\mathbf d) = \sum_{\mathbf x \in W} \big( I(\mathbf x) - J(\mathbf x + \mathbf d) \big)^2 . $$

Linearising

Write $\mathbf d = \mathbf d_k + \boldsymbol\delta$, where $\mathbf d_k$ is the current estimate and $\boldsymbol\delta$ a small correction, and Taylor-expand $J$ around $\mathbf x + \mathbf d_k$:

$$ J(\mathbf x + \mathbf d_k + \boldsymbol\delta) \approx J(\mathbf x + \mathbf d_k) + \nabla J(\mathbf x + \mathbf d_k)^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\delta \approx J(\mathbf x + \mathbf d_k) + \nabla I(\mathbf x)^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol\delta . $$

The last step uses $\nabla J \approx \nabla I$, which holds once the two patches are aligned. It also means the gradients only have to be computed once. Define the per-pixel difference $\delta I_k(\mathbf x) = I(\mathbf x) - J(\mathbf x + \mathbf d_k)$. Then

$$ \varepsilon(\boldsymbol\delta) \approx \sum_{W} \big( \delta I_k - I_x\delta_x - I_y\delta_y \big)^2 . $$

Set the derivative to zero:

$$ \frac{\partial\varepsilon}{\partial\boldsymbol\delta} = -2\sum_W \begin{bmatrix} I_x \\ I_y \end{bmatrix}\big(\delta I_k - I_x\delta_x - I_y\delta_y\big) = \mathbf 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \sum I_x^2 & \sum I_xI_y \\ \sum I_xI_y & \sum I_y^2 \end{bmatrix}}_{G} \boldsymbol\delta = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \sum \delta I_k\, I_x \\ \sum \delta I_k\, I_y \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf b_k} . $$

So each step solves the 2 × 2 linear system $G\boldsymbol\delta = \mathbf b_k$ and updates the estimate (a Newton-Raphson / Gauss-Newton iteration):

$$ \mathbf d_{k+1} = \mathbf d_k + G^{-1}\mathbf b_k, \qquad \mathbf d_0 = \mathbf 0, \qquad \text{stop when } \lVert G^{-1}\mathbf b_k\rVert < 0.01\ \text{px}. $$

With $\mathbf d_k = \mathbf 0$ and $I_t = J - I = -\delta I_0$, the first step is exactly the least-squares solution of the flow constraint $I_x u + I_y v + I_t = 0$ stacked over the window, so this is equation $\eqref{ofce}$ solved properly. The tracked position in the second frame is

$$ \mathbf u' = \mathbf u + \mathbf d . $$

This predicted location is what part 2 checks against the actual pixel location.

When can a point be tracked?

$G$ (the structure tensor) has to be invertible and well conditioned. With eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2$:

  • both small - a flat patch, nothing to track;
  • $\lambda_1 \gg \lambda_2 \approx 0$ - an edge, only the normal component is determined (the aperture problem again);
  • both large - a corner, $\mathbf d$ is well determined.

That is why we track Shi-Tomasi corners, which are the points that maximise $\lambda_2$ ("good features to track").

Large motion: pyramids

The Taylor expansion only holds for displacements of about a pixel or two. For larger motion, build Gaussian pyramids $I^L, J^L$ (each level half the size of the one below, $L = 0\ldots3$), solve at the coarsest level where the motion is small, and pass the estimate down:

$$ \mathbf g^{L_{\max}} = \mathbf 0, \qquad \mathbf g^{L-1} = 2\,(\mathbf g^L + \mathbf d^L), \qquad \mathbf d = \mathbf g^0 + \mathbf d^0 , $$

where $\mathbf d^L$ is the result of the iteration above at level $L$, run on $J^L(\mathbf x + \mathbf g^L + \mathbf d_k)$.

Where interpolation comes in

$\mathbf x + \mathbf g + \mathbf d_k$ is almost never an integer pixel position, so every iteration has to sample $J$ between pixels. The same goes for the window around $\mathbf u / 2^L$ on the coarse levels. The code does this with bilinear interpolation, derived next.

4. Bilinear interpolation

We want $f(x, y)$ at a non-integer point. Let $x_0 = \lfloor x\rfloor$, $y_0 = \lfloor y\rfloor$ and the fractional parts $a = x - x_0$, $b = y - y_0$ ($0 \le a, b < 1$). The four surrounding pixels are

$$ f_{00} = f(x_0, y_0),\quad f_{10} = f(x_0{+}1, y_0),\quad f_{01} = f(x_0, y_0{+}1),\quad f_{11} = f(x_0{+}1, y_0{+}1). $$

Step 1 - linear interpolation along $x$ on both rows. On the line through two points the value changes linearly, so

$$ f(x, y_0) = (1-a) f_{00} + a f_{10}, \qquad f(x, y_0{+}1) = (1-a) f_{01} + a f_{11}. $$

Step 2 - linear interpolation along $y$ between those two results:

$$ f(x, y) = (1-b)\, f(x, y_0) + b\, f(x, y_0{+}1) = (1-a)(1-b) f_{00} + a(1-b) f_{10} + (1-a)b f_{01} + ab f_{11}. $$

In matrix form:

$$ f(x, y) = \begin{bmatrix} 1-b & b \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} f_{00} & f_{10} \\ f_{01} & f_{11} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1-a \\ a \end{bmatrix}. $$

Doing $y$ first and then $x$ gives the same result (the matrix product is associative), so the order doesn't matter. The same formula comes out of a second route. Ask for the function of the form $f = c_0 + c_1 a + c_2 b + c_3 ab$ (linear in each variable separately) that passes through the four corners. Plugging in $(a,b) = (0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (1,1)$ gives

$$ c_0 = f_{00},\quad c_1 = f_{10} - f_{00},\quad c_2 = f_{01} - f_{00},\quad c_3 = f_{11} - f_{10} - f_{01} + f_{00}, $$

and expanding recovers the expression above.

Properties worth knowing:

  • The four weights $(1-a)(1-b)$, $a(1-b)$, $(1-a)b$, $ab$ are the areas of the sub-rectangles opposite each corner. They are non-negative and sum to 1, so the result is a weighted average and never overshoots the neighbours.
  • It reproduces constant and linear (planar) intensity exactly, is continuous across pixel borders, and its error is $O(h^2 f'')$.
  • Its derivative jumps at pixel borders (it is $C^0$, not $C^1$). That's why the tracker takes gradients from a smoothed derivative filter and then interpolates those, rather than differentiating the interpolant.

The tracking page shows the formula evaluated at the tracked position of one feature. It is checked against OpenCV's getRectSubPix, which uses the same interpolation.

5. Structure from motion with a planar object

Camera model

A point $\mathbf X$ in camera-1 coordinates projects into view $i$ as

$$ \lambda_i \tilde{\mathbf x}_i = K\,(R_i\mathbf X + \mathbf t_i), \qquad K = \begin{bmatrix} f_x & 0 & c_x \\ 0 & f_y & c_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, $$

with $R_1 = I$, $\mathbf t_1 = \mathbf 0$ and $\lambda_i$ the depth. $K$ and the lens distortion come from the HW2 calibration. We work in normalised coordinates $\mathbf m_i = K^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf x}_i$ (after undistortion), so that $\lambda_i\mathbf m_i = R_i\mathbf X + \mathbf t_i$.

Why a homography

With a general 3-D scene one would estimate the essential matrix from 8+ points. For a flat object that estimate is degenerate: all points lie on one plane, and the plane admits a whole family of essential matrices. The plane is also what makes the problem easy, though. Write it in camera-1 coordinates as $\mathbf N^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X = d$, with $\mathbf N$ the unit normal and $d > 0$ the distance from camera 1. For every point on the plane $\mathbf N^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X / d = 1$, so

$$ \lambda_i\mathbf m_i = R_i\mathbf X + \mathbf t_i\,\frac{\mathbf N^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X}{d} = \Big(R_i + \frac{1}{d}\mathbf t_i\mathbf N^{\mathsf T}\Big)\mathbf X = \Big(R_i + \frac{1}{d}\mathbf t_i\mathbf N^{\mathsf T}\Big)\lambda_1\mathbf m_1 . $$

So views 1 and $i$ are related by the plane-induced homography

$$ \mathbf m_i \sim H_i\,\mathbf m_1, \qquad H_i = R_i + \mathbf T_i\mathbf N^{\mathsf T},\quad \mathbf T_i = \mathbf t_i/d, \qquad\text{(in pixels: } \tilde{\mathbf x}_i \sim K H_i K^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf x}_1). $$

Only $\mathbf t_i/d$ appears: from images alone the scene is known up to one global scale. We set $d = 1$ and fix the scale at the end with one measured length.

Step 1 - estimating $H_i$ (DLT)

$\mathbf m_i \sim H\mathbf m_1$ means $\mathbf m_i \times H\mathbf m_1 = \mathbf 0$. Write $\mathbf m_1 = (x, y, 1)$, $\mathbf m_i = (x', y', 1)$ and $\mathbf h$ = the 9 entries of $H$ row by row. Two of the three rows are independent:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} -x & -y & -1 & 0 & 0 & 0 & x'x & x'y & x' \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & -x & -y & -1 & y'x & y'y & y' \end{bmatrix} \mathbf h = \mathbf 0 . $$

Stack the rows for all $n \ge 4$ points into $A$ ($2n \times 9$). Minimise $\lVert A\mathbf h\rVert$ subject to $\lVert\mathbf h\rVert = 1$; the solution is the right singular vector of $A$ with the smallest singular value. Before this, the points are shifted to zero mean and scaled to mean distance $\sqrt2$ (Hartley normalisation, $\hat{\mathbf m} = T\mathbf m$), which keeps $A$ well conditioned. Afterwards $H = T_i^{-1}\hat H T_1$.

Step 2 - decomposing $H = R + \mathbf T\mathbf N^{\mathsf T}$

$H$ from the DLT has an unknown scale. The middle singular value of $R + \mathbf T\mathbf N^{\mathsf T}$ is always exactly 1, so divide by $\sigma_2(H)$. Choose the sign so that $(H\mathbf m_1)_z > 0$ (both depths positive). Then (Ma, Soatto, Košecká & Sastry, §5.3) take the eigen-decomposition $H^{\mathsf T}H = V\,\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1^2, 1, \sigma_3^2)\,V^{\mathsf T}$, $V = [\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2, \mathbf v_3]$, $\det V = 1$. $\mathbf v_2$ is the direction whose length $H$ preserves, and the two unit vectors

$$ \mathbf u_{1,2} = \frac{\sqrt{1-\sigma_3^2}\,\mathbf v_1 \pm \sqrt{\sigma_1^2-1}\,\mathbf v_3}{\sqrt{\sigma_1^2-\sigma_3^2}} $$

also keep their length. Since $H\mathbf a = R\mathbf a$ for every $\mathbf a \perp \mathbf N$, the plane spanned by $\mathbf v_2$ and $\mathbf u_j$ can be the object plane. With $U_j = [\mathbf v_2,\ \mathbf u_j,\ \mathbf v_2\times\mathbf u_j]$ and $W_j = [H\mathbf v_2,\ H\mathbf u_j,\ H\mathbf v_2 \times H\mathbf u_j]$:

$$ R_j = W_jU_j^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \mathbf N_j = \mathbf v_2 \times \mathbf u_j, \qquad \mathbf T_j = (H - R_j)\mathbf N_j , $$

plus $(R_j, -\mathbf T_j, -\mathbf N_j)$. That makes four candidates per homography, and two tests leave one:

  1. Positive depth - every point must be in front of camera 1: $\mathbf N^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X = d > 0$ with $\mathbf X = \lambda_1\mathbf m_1$, so $\mathbf N^{\mathsf T}\mathbf m_1 > 0$ for all points. This removes two of the four.
  2. One plane, many views - $H_2, H_3, H_4$ all see the same plane, so their $\mathbf N$ (in camera-1 coordinates) must agree. We choose the combination with the smallest total angle between normals. This is where having four views pays off: with only two views the remaining ambiguity can't be resolved from the geometry.

Step 3 - triangulation

Now $P_i = [R_i \mid \mathbf T_i]$ is known for every view (in normalised coordinates). For a point, $\lambda_i\mathbf m_i = P_i\tilde{\mathbf X}$. Taking the cross product with $\mathbf m_i$ removes $\lambda_i$ and leaves two linear equations per view ($\mathbf p_i^{k\mathsf T}$ is row $k$ of $P_i$):

$$ \begin{bmatrix} x_i\,\mathbf p_i^{3\mathsf T} - \mathbf p_i^{1\mathsf T} \\ y_i\,\mathbf p_i^{3\mathsf T} - \mathbf p_i^{2\mathsf T} \end{bmatrix} \tilde{\mathbf X} = \mathbf 0 ,\qquad i = 1\ldots4 \;\Rightarrow\; A\tilde{\mathbf X} = \mathbf 0,\ A \in \mathbb R^{8\times4}. $$

$\tilde{\mathbf X}$ is again the smallest right singular vector of $A$, then divided by its 4th coordinate. Because the point is on the plane, there's also a one-view shortcut we use as a check: $\mathbf X = \mathbf m_1 / (\mathbf N^{\mathsf T}\mathbf m_1)$ (with $d = 1$).

Step 4 - bundle adjustment

The linear steps minimise algebraic errors, not distances in the image. Finally all poses (view 1 fixed) and all points are refined together by minimising the reprojection error in pixels (Levenberg-Marquardt style least squares):

$$ \min_{\{R_i,\mathbf T_i\},\{\mathbf X_j\}} \sum_{i=1}^{4}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \big\lVert \tilde{\mathbf x}_{ij} - \pi\big(K(R_i\mathbf X_j + \mathbf T_i)\big) \big\rVert^2, \qquad \pi(x, y, z) = (x/z,\ y/z). $$

Step 5 - scale, plane and boundary

  • Scale: if the true distance between points $a$ and $b$ is $L$ cm, then $s = L / \lVert\mathbf X_a - \mathbf X_b\rVert$ cm per unit. Every length is multiplied by $s$.
  • Plane: fit a plane to the points by SVD (normal = singular vector of the centred points with the smallest singular value). The RMS distance from it shows how flat the reconstruction came out.
  • Object frame: origin at point $a$, $x$-axis along $a \to b$, $z$-axis the normal towards the camera, $y = z \times x$. In this frame each point has coordinates $(u_j, v_j, \approx 0)$ - that's the boundary in the object's own 2-D coordinates.
  • Boundary: the points are clicked in order around the edge, so the boundary is the polygon through them. Edge lengths are $\lVert(u,v)_{j+1} - (u,v)_j\rVert$, and the area comes from the shoelace formula $A = \tfrac12\left|\sum_j (u_j v_{j+1} - u_{j+1}v_j)\right|$.
  • Camera positions: camera $i$'s centre satisfies $R_i\mathbf C_i + \mathbf T_i = \mathbf 0$, so $\mathbf C_i = -R_i^{\mathsf T}\mathbf T_i$, then it is moved into the object frame and scaled.

With the plane known, view 1 can also be re-rendered as seen straight from above. A point $(u, v)$ on the object is at $\mathbf X = \mathbf X_a + \tfrac{u}{s}\mathbf e_1 + \tfrac{v}{s}\mathbf e_2$ in camera 1, so it appears at pixel $K[\mathbf e_1/s \;\; \mathbf e_2/s \;\; \mathbf X_a](u, v, 1)^{\mathsf T}$ - another homography, which is used to warp the photo.
